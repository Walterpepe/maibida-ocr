How to Choose a Mold Protection Monitor: Camera, Lighting, Cycle and ROI

In short: do not choose a mold protection monitor by pixel count alone. Match it across four dimensions—mold complexity, part and material, molding cycle, and the number of inspection items—then size the camera resolution and channels, lighting, inspection speed and ROI strategy, and validate sensitivity through trial runs to combine reliable collision protection with low false alarms.

1. Define Four Basic Conditions First

A mold protection monitor works closely with the injection machine and the mold, so the first step is clarifying shop-floor conditions rather than comparing spec sheets:

  • Mold structure: number of cavities, presence of slides/inserts/ejectors, multi-material or insert molding;
  • Part and material: plastic color, reflectivity, minimum defect size, and the contrast of the part inside the mold;
  • Molding cycle: cycle time, which sets the time window available for capture and judgment;
  • Inspection items: only residue/sticking, or also short fill, flash, dimensions and color mixing.

2. Camera Resolution and Channel Count

Resolution determines the smallest detectable feature; channel count determines how many viewing angles one controller covers.

Resolution: derive from the smallest feature

Industrial cameras commonly range from about 2 to 8 megapixels. The rule is ensuring the smallest feature (fine ejectors, small inserts, tiny short fill) occupies enough pixels, rather than choosing the highest pixel count. Higher resolution means more data and greater demands on judgment speed and storage.

Channels: size by viewing angle

A single camera fits single-cavity, single-angle molds; multi-cavity molds or simultaneous fixed/moving/side views need a multi-channel controller (MAIBIDA controllers offer multiple Ethernet ports for extra cameras) to inspect several angles within one cycle.

3. Lighting: Contrast Drives Inspection Accuracy

Whether the camera can see a defect depends heavily on lighting. Common selection logic:

  • White light: suits most standard plastic parts and preserves color information;
  • Infrared light: penetrates some oil and dust, resists ambient light variation, suits dark cavities or fluctuating shop lighting;
  • Integrated dual light: switchable IR/white in one unit covers more materials and scenes and reduces later retrofit.

Ask the supplier to run trials with your actual parts and molds to confirm sufficient contrast among the part, residue and background.

4. Match Inspection Speed to the Molding Cycle

The monitor must finish capture and judgment before closing begins, otherwise it cannot protect the mold. Confirm:

  1. Trigger source: prefer the machine’s ‘mold fully open’ signal rather than an in-motion signal to avoid blurred images;
  2. Capture delay: wait for vibration to settle and the robot to leave; thick-wall or slow molds need a longer delay;
  3. Judgment time: single-region time must be far below the available window (MAIBIDA reaches about 0.03 s per region), with margin for I/O interlock.

5. ROI and Sensitivity: Balancing Misses and False Alarms

ROI (region) principles

Frame only the areas that truly need monitoring—cavities, ejectors, slides—keeping them ‘as small as practical’, and mask normal-variation areas such as wear marks, venting grooves and robot paths to avoid persistent false alarms.

Sensitivity (difference threshold)

  • Too high: alarms on minor differences, causing frequent stops;
  • Too low: real residue may be missed—the biggest risk;
  • Correct approach: fine-tune each region over 10–30 trial cycles; each ROI can have independent sensitivity and inspection area instead of a single global setting.

6. Software and Scalability Matter Too

Beyond hardware, evaluate support for arbitrary-shape ROI, independent user passwords to prevent misoperation, automatic brightness correction, alarm image/parameter export, multi-camera centralized monitoring and MES integration. These determine long-term stability and traceability.

FAQ

Is a higher-pixel mold protection monitor always better?

No. Resolution should be derived from the smallest defect to detect, ensuring key features occupy enough pixels; excessive pixels add data volume and judgment time. Evaluate contrast, lighting, cycle time and ROI together.

How do I choose between single- and multi-camera monitors?

Single camera fits single-cavity, single-angle molds. Multi-cavity molds or simultaneous fixed/moving/side views need a multi-channel controller with several cameras to cover multiple angles in one cycle.

How do I choose between infrared and white lighting?

White light suits most standard plastic parts and keeps color data; infrared resists ambient light, oil and dust better, suiting dark cavities or fluctuating shop light. A switchable IR/white dual-light unit is more versatile—confirm through trials.

What ROI sensitivity value should I use?

There is no fixed value. Fine-tune region by region over 10–30 trial cycles, avoiding missed residue while limiting false alarms. Each ROI can have independent sensitivity and inspection area, with wear and venting areas masked.

Why does judgment speed matter in selection?

Because the monitor must finish judgment and output the interlock signal before closing to prevent collision. Combine trigger signal, capture delay and per-region judgment time to ensure total time is well within the available cycle window.
